4. Watch the first batch like a hawk

Automation is obedient, not wise. If the first ten rows or first five emails look wrong, stop before the rest run. Pair this with how you stop an actor and advisor vs actor.

Write down who may enable bulk actions. One curious “run for all” click can turn a tidy-up into a week of apologies.

Automation feels like momentum. You set a rule, approve a plan, or tell a tool to “finish the rest,” and the little progress bar becomes a kind of comfort.

The risk is not that machines are rebellious. It is that they are obedient — and they will obediently repeat a wrong step a hundred times while you are not watching.

Confidence is not the same as correctness. If it sounds sure, that is when you check the first three results — not the last.

1. A Tuesday that got too tidy

You ask a helper to rename files, tag emails, or update a spreadsheet “for everything that matches.” It starts well. Then you notice twenty folders named after a typo you typed once, or customers marked “paid” because a column looked similar.

Nothing crashed. The automation kept going. You only stopped it when the mess became visible.

2. Where runaway loops hide

  • Bulk email sends and “follow up everyone” flows
  • Auto-filing rules that catch the wrong subject line
  • Scripts that overwrite files without a backup
  • Agents told to “keep trying until it works”

3. Guardrails that still feel light

  • Run on a small sample first (ten items, not ten thousand).
  • Prefer preview / dry-run modes.
  • Keep version history or backups on before bulk edits.
  • Set a human pause for anything public, financial, or customer-facing.

Watch the first batch. If the first three outputs are right, continue. If any look odd, stop — do not “let it finish.”
