Phishing is a message designed to make you act before you think — click, sign in, send money, or share a code. Brands look familiar. The pressure is the point.

You do not need to catch every trick. You need a slow habit for anything that wants credentials or cash.

Never enter a password from a link in an unexpected message — open the site yourself from a bookmark or typed address.

1. Common UK shapes

Fake bank or HMRC urgency, parcel “fees,” prize claims, IT password resets, invoice PDFs with a twist. Compensations and “your device is compromised” surveys are frequent guests.

2. Checks that still work

  • Hover or long-press to preview the real URL
  • Look for urgency + secrecy together
  • Compare the sender domain carefully (rnicrosoft-style tricks)
  • Use a known app or typed address for banking
  • Report and delete — don’t “test” the link

3. If you clicked

Change the password from a different device if you can. Turn on MFA. Check forwarding rules. Tell your bank if money or card details were involved. Report via Action Fraud.

Deeper reads: spotting fake email, scams hub, what to do if you’ve been caught out.

4. Build a boring reporting habit

In a small team, decide where suspect messages go — a shared mailbox, a chat channel, or one named person. Speed beats embarrassment. The goal is not a perfect forensic report; it is stopping the next person clicking the same lure.

At home, screenshot and tell someone before you engage. If money moved, call the bank on a number from the card or known app, not from the email. Then report through Action Fraud so the pattern is visible beyond your inbox.

Keep a short personal blocklist of moods that get you in trouble: tired Sunday nights, rushing for a parcel, anything that says “your account will lock in 15 minutes.” Those moods are when phishing works best. Plan your pause for them.

Unexpected link + password ask = stop. Navigate yourself.
