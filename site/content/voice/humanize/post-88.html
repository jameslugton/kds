Weak passwords are still one of the quietest ways accounts get taken over — not because attackers are clever, but because reused logins turn one breach into many.

This is the home and small-business version: unique passwords where it matters, a manager that fits real life, and MFA on the accounts that reset everything else.

One strong unique password per important account beats a clever phrase reused everywhere.

1. Where households and micro-teams leak

The same password on shopping, email, and the till admin. A note on the fridge that outlives a housemate. Browser-saved logins on a shared laptop. A “temporary” password that became permanent.

2. A practical stack

  • Password manager for new and important logins
  • Unique passwords for email, banking, accounting, and admin panels
  • MFA on email first — it resets everything else
  • Share deliberately with the manager’s share features, not by text

3. When life changes

Someone moves out, leaves the business, or loses a phone: change the shared household or shop logins the same week. Recovery codes belong offline, not in the same inbox an attacker just opened.

For family cloud IDs, see the scheduled piece on shared logins. For a full SMB baseline, use the 2026 checklist (PDF).

4. A Saturday morning reset

Pick coffee, not crisis. Install or open a reputable password manager. Save the email login first, then banking, then the shop or work admin panel. Turn on MFA while you still have the phone in hand. Change any password you know you’ve reused — even if “nothing bad has happened yet.” Reuse is the quiet compromise; the breach email is only the day you find out.

If a partner or colleague needs access, share the vault entry properly rather than reading the password aloud. When they leave, revoke that share the same day you collect keys or laptop chargers. That is the household version of joiner-mover-leaver — undramatic and effective.

Store recovery codes on paper or in an offline place the password manager recommends. Do not leave them only in the inbox that MFA is supposed to protect. If you ever lose the phone, you want a path that does not depend on the attacker already reading your mail.

Start with email + banking + a password manager. Widen from there.
