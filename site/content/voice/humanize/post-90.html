Social engineering is the human pattern behind most scams: urgency, authority, fear, helpfulness, and secrecy. AI can polish the sentences. It does not remove your right to pause.

This guide is the short pattern check — pair it with the newer piece on how AI amplifies the same levers.

Name the lever out loud — urgency, authority, secrecy — then verify before you comply.

1. The levers

  • Authority: “this is the bank / IT / a supplier”
  • Urgency: “act now or lose access”
  • Fear: “your account will close”
  • Helpfulness: “I’ll fix it if you just…”
  • Isolation: “don’t tell anyone — it’ll slow us down”

2. A vignette

A calm caller knows a staff name and needs a “quick approval code.” An hour later, invoice rules have changed. Nothing exotic — a person was hurried into being helpful.

3. Pattern check

  • Who benefits if I rush?
  • Can I verify on a number or site I already trust?
  • Would this still make sense tomorrow morning?
  • Am I being asked to keep this secret from people I normally tell?

4. Train the pause, not the panic

Teams remember stories better than policy PDFs. Once a quarter, bring one real message you almost trusted — an invoice change, a delivery fee, an IT reset — and ask which lever it pulled. Celebrate the person who forwarded it for a second look. Shame is how social engineering wins twice.

At home, agree a family code word for money emergencies and a rule that surprise gift-card or transfer requests get a callback on a known number. The same pattern that protects a till protects a parent.

When AI writes the bait, the grammar improves; the levers do not change. If the message wants speed and secrecy, treat fluency as costume, not proof. Then verify.

Pause is still the defence. Verify on a channel you already trust.
