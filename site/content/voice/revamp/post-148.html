Ever received an email that looked too real to be fake? Maybe a message claiming to be from your bank, asking you to “verify your account details” right away? That is social engineering — fraudsters manipulating people into handing over private information, no fancy hacking required.

Unlike movie-style cyberattacks, these scams target people using deception, urgency, and fear to get you to act before you think.

How scammers trick you

  • Phishing emails and messages — look like banks, shops, or your workplace but contain fake links
  • Impersonation calls — someone pretends to be IT support, customer service, or a government body with an “urgent problem”
  • Baiting — fake job offers, “free giveaways,” or even a USB stick left in a car park
  • Physical tricks — following someone through a secure door or pretending to be delivery staff

What helps

  • Pause before you click — urgency is a red flag, not a reason to hurry
  • Verify directly — hang up and call the official number from a card or statement
  • Unique passwords and mfa — so a stolen login does not open everything
  • Limit what you share publicly — job titles and travel plans help impersonators dress up
  • Say something — report odd attempts and warn others

At the moment of risk

Stop! Pause! Is it urgent? Do you know who it is from? Is it really from who it says it is? Unusual requests deserve a verify — through a channel you already trust, not the one that started the conversation.

A self-check

Which channel catches you off guard most — email, phone, text, or social media? Pick that one and decide now what you will do the next time someone asks for details, money, or codes through it: verify first, every time.
