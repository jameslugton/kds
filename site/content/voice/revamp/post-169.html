An email that looks like your bank — “Urgent: suspicious activity” — is designed to make you move before you think. Phishing has always worked that way. Deepfakes add another polish to voice and video.

You do not need special tools to respond well: pause, verify through a channel you already trust, and do not use the links or numbers in the message.

Common phishing tricks

  • Email phishing — fake bank or shop messages with suspicious links
  • Text scams (smishing) — “Your package is delayed, click here” when there is no package
  • Voice scams (vishing) — callers pretending to be tech support, banks, or family in distress
  • Fake social accounts — scammers posing as brands, influencers, or friends
  • Clone websites — pages that look identical to the real login except they steal your details

Deepfakes — voice and video fakes

  • Fake voice calls — a cloned boss asking for a wire transfer
  • Fake video messages — “your manager” on a call that is not really them
  • Ai chat scams — bots pretending to be customer service

The polish is new. The levers are not: urgency, authority, secrecy.

At the moment of risk

Stop! Pause! Is it urgent? Do you know who it is from? Is it really from who it says it is? If a voice or video feels right but the request is odd — money, passwords, codes, secrecy — call back on a number you already trust. Not the one in the message.

What helps

  • Verify out of band — separate channel, known number
  • Hover or long-press links before you click
  • Mfa on email and banking
  • Treat urgency as a warning sign, not a reason to hurry

A self-check

If you got a voice note right now from someone you know asking for an urgent transfer — would you send it, or would you ring them back first? That split-second answer is the habit worth keeping.
