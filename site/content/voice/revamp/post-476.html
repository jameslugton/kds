This one landed dressed as an urgent Gmail security alert. Your “subscription has closed,” it says. Final notice. Account terminated in 48 hours unless you renew. Big button: “Secure Your Device.”

It is not from Google. Gmail is free — there is no membership to renew. It is a common fake-email scam aimed at your Google login.

What it claimed

  • Gmail security alert and “final notice”
  • Your device is “unprotected” — act within 48 hours
  • A fake account ID, your username, a “90% discount,” limited-time date
  • Signature from “Gmail Support Team”

Why it is bait

Google does not send emails like this. The tells stack up fast:

  • Urgency — “Final warning,” 48-hour deadline, last chance
  • Fear — unprotected account, hackers, cyber attacks
  • Fake subscription talk — Gmail has nothing to renew
  • Made-up details — random account ID, “90% off” security (Google does not do that)
  • Wrong button — “Secure Your Device” instead of a link to google.com or gmail.com

Click through and you usually hit a fake Google login page, a fake renewal screen, or a form after your password and payment details. A stolen Google account is high value — email, contacts, cloud storage, backup codes, password resets for other sites.

Stop. Pause. Ask three questions.

Before you tap that button:

  • Is it urgent?
  • Do you know who it is from?
  • Is it really from who it is from?

Final notice, 48 hours, account closure — that is urgency by design. The display name may say Gmail; the real sender address will not be Google. If you are not sure, it is likely a catch. Do not click. Do not sign in. Do not pay.

What to do instead

  • Delete the email. Block the sender.
  • If you want to check your account, open gmail.com yourself — type it or use a bookmark. Never through a link in the message.
  • Look at recent sign-in activity inside your real Google account settings.

Already clicked or typed your password? Change your Google password straight away, update recovery details, turn on multi-factor authentication (mfa), review connected devices, and contact your bank if you entered card details. Step-by-step help: what to do after a miss.

A self-check

When did you last sign into Gmail through the app or by typing the address yourself — not through a link in an email? If you cannot remember, that is exactly the gap these messages exploit. Next “security alert” that lands: would you open the app first, or reach for the button?
